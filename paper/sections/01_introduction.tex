\section{Introduction}

Charts make quantitative relations visible, but they also make scale a moving target. The same visual evidence can be verbalized as a percentage, a decimal, a raw value, or a value in thousands or millions. A chart question answering model must therefore do more than find the relevant bar, line, or label: it must decide which scale the answer should use.

Modern multimodal large language models (MLLMs) achieve strong aggregate accuracy on chart QA benchmarks such as ChartQA \citep{masry2022chartqa}. Aggregate accuracy can nevertheless hide rare failures that are costly when they occur. We study \emph{scale inconsistency}: a model gives a numerically related answer under the wrong scale, such as predicting \textit{0.61} when the chart question requires \textit{61}. This is not just a generic numeric miss. The model has often found a related quantity, but has failed at the last reliability step: choosing the scale in which to express it.

The obvious fix is tempting and wrong. One might normalize all percentages, train an adapter that prefers larger numbers, or ask the model to self-check units. Each helps some cases and hurts others. A legitimate answer such as \textit{0.0593} should not be expanded into \textit{6.0333}; an axis in millions should not be treated like a percent display; and a prediction that is already too large needs the opposite intervention from a prediction that is too small. The central question is therefore not simply whether a model can produce a scaled alternative. It is whether a system can select the correct \emph{scale regime}: the direction, unit context, and output convention required by the chart and question.

Natural strict scale errors are sparse under any single model and split, so the evidence must be read at two levels. Across the available ChartQA validation/test outputs we audit, strict factor-of-100 errors account for 0.12--1.09\% of examples (Table~\ref{tab:strict_prevalence}); our original ChartQA validation analysis contains 23 such examples. We use these subsets as natural recovery probes, not as independent benchmarks. To understand the mechanism behind those sparse failures, we construct ScaleQA-Natural, a mined, machine-audited, author-adjudicated, and independently inclusion-audited set of 520 natural scale-conflict candidates. ScaleQA-Natural is deliberately framed as a diagnostic set, not as a replacement for official ChartQA evaluation.

Our main empirical finding is that scale correction is directional and regime-dependent. In an answer-tuned Qwen2.5-VL-7B setting, a large-positive scale-contrastive adapter improves held-out ChartQA test accuracy from 88.60 to 89.32 and recovers 49.52\% of strict test-set gold-scale errors. This +0.72 point gain is real, but it is not the paper's main claim: it is measured after answer tuning and should not be confused with the much larger raw-to-answer-tuned improvement. The more important result appears when the same idea is stress-tested. On ScaleQA-Natural, a self-check prompt and a lightweight no-leak policy selector outperform the global adapter. The selector's gain over self-check is modest but stable across held-out splits, while high candidate oracles show that the correct scaled answer is often present in the candidate pool. The bottleneck is selection.

This changes the role of adaptation in the paper. We use LoRA adapters as diagnostic probes for directional preferences, not as a universal repair mechanism. Their successes and failures reveal when a scale preference is helpful, when it is harmful, and why unconditional correction is unsafe. Cross-backbone and external numeric checks reinforce the same lesson: the phenomenon transfers more robustly than the intervention.

\noindent\textbf{Contributions.}
We contribute (i) a strict natural scale-error recovery probe and ScaleQA-Natural, an audited diagnostic set for natural scale conflicts;
(ii) evidence that correction is directional: a large-positive adapter repairs nearly half of strict held-out test scale errors, but is unsafe as a global policy;
(iii) a comparison of prompts, verifiers, calculators, likelihood scoring, and no-leak selectors that separates candidate generation from selection; and
(iv) a persistent selection bottleneck: the best deployable selector only modestly beats self-check, with large oracle gaps remaining.
