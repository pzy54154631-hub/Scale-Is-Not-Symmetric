\section*{Limitations}

\paragraph{Scope of the scale definition.}
Our strongest evidence concerns factor-of-100 percentage--decimal errors in ChartQA-derived data; 92.8\% of image-evaluable ScaleQA-Natural items involve explicit percent evidence. Factors of 10 and 1000, currency units, scientific notation, logarithmic axes, percentage-point differences, and mixed units appear only in small groups and receive no systematic evaluation. Claims about ``scale reliability'' in general should be read as claims about this regime until the evaluation is broadened.

\paragraph{Small verified slices.}
The verified strict test subset contains \verify{17} examples, so one example corresponds to roughly six percentage points; we report counts with Wilson intervals and treat recovery as diagnostic-slice evidence, not a population estimate. ScaleQA-Natural is a mined, author-adjudicated set; the inclusion audit reviewed retained positives only and does not bound false negatives in the rejected pool.

\paragraph{Selector evaluation.}
Selector variants were iterated on the same diagnostic collection with repeated held-out-fold evaluation rather than a pre-registered test partition or nested cross-validation, so the reported advantage over self-check is exposed to researcher-level overfitting; the leave-source-out analysis is our mitigation, and it shows the advantage is fragile. The selector also requires chart-context extraction, auxiliary prompts, and calibration whose latency and cost we have not characterized, so we do not call it deployable.

\paragraph{Models and transfer.}
The main positive result uses Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct in an answer-tuned setting. InternVL2.5-1B/8B/26B diagnostics and PlotQA, ChartBench, MathVista, and InfographicVQA subsets use differing prompts, subset sizes, and sample definitions; they show that ratio-related errors occur elsewhere, not that prevalence or taxonomy is stable across models. None of these subsets is a full-benchmark or state-of-the-art comparison.

\paragraph{Deployment risk.}
Incorrect scale interpretation can be consequential in financial, scientific, policy, or medical charts, and the present results do not establish that any tested intervention is safe in those settings. Silently rescaling a numerically plausible answer can convert a visible error into an invisible one. Benchmark accuracy also differs from semantic correctness: \textit{0.61} and \textit{61\%} may be equivalent where the unit is explicit and seriously wrong where it is not. We recommend against deploying automatic scale correction policies on the basis of these results.
